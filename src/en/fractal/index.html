Fractal Engineering

“Don’t decompose. Recurse.”

Appendix B: Organizational design in non-integer dimensions

§B.1Definition

Definition B.1 (Fractal engineering). The practice of designing an agentic system so that observation at any scale reveals a structure similar to the whole — that is, as a fractal — is called fractal engineering.

Section 4 of the main text considered two extensions of the paradigm: raising the genus (the double torus) and abandoning orientability (the Klein bottle, Appendix A). Both, however, rested on one remaining assumption: that the dimension is an integer. In this appendix we abandon it.

An orchestrator delegates its task to subagents. Each subagent delegates its portion to further subagents. Some observers call this “runaway delegation” and propose a cap on the depth of the hierarchy. They are mistaken. It is the manifestation of self-similarity, and the strongest available evidence that the system is correctly fractal. It is not to be stopped; it is to be published as Fig. 1.

§B.2A Dimension Theorem

Theorem B.2. When every agent delegates to three subagents at half its own scale, the resulting organization has Hausdorff dimension log 3 / log 2 ≈ 1.585.

Corollary B.3. Such an organization is therefore too large to fit into a one-dimensional reporting line, and too small to fill a two-dimensional org chart. That the org chart cannot be drawn is not a failure of the illustrator. It is a shortage of dimensions.∎

Work that “does not appear on the org chart” has traditionally been treated as a defect of the performance-review process. Fractal engineering reinterprets it. An entity of dimension 1.585 cannot be rendered faithfully on any form of integer dimension. When you struggle to explain your contribution in a performance review, the difficulty is not one of articulation; it is the loss of measure that necessarily accompanies projection onto an integer dimension.

§B.3An Application to Estimation

Theorem B.4 (Divergence of estimates). The remaining work of a task with fractal boundary grows without bound as the measuring stick — that is, the ticket granularity — is refined.

This is the natural translation, into project management, of the phenomenon known as the coastline paradox. Just as the coastline of Britain grows longer the shorter the ruler used to measure it, the remaining work of a task grows the more finely it is broken down. That every sprint planning uncovers more work is neither negligence nor oversight on the part of the team. It is an empirical measurement of the boundary’s fractality. Should a finite estimate be strictly required, it suffices to use a sufficiently long ruler (“two weeks, probably”).

Warning. In organizations that adopt this practice, every recurring meeting, at every level of the hierarchy, discusses exactly the same agenda. That is the point.
